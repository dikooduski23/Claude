**Why this list exists:** our two flops (Lottery Man 0.14x, Stratton's glasses 0.18x) were stories that had already gone viral elsewhere. Every story here fits the formula (it looked stupid on purpose, then a hidden trick paid off) and has a mechanism we can show in a cutaway. None has a big viral short that I could find, and the key facts have 2+ sources. The hook scores are first drafts from `ig-reel hookscore.py`; each one gets reworked to 70+ when its script is written (the Dutch Water Line went from 38 to 78 that way).

**Rejected:** the Indiana Bell building rotated with 600 people inside (went viral in 2021 on LADbible, Open Culture and Neatorama). Chicago lifting the Tremont Hotel (dates conflict between sources; viral-ish).

Obscurity is my judgment (★★★ = almost nobody has heard it).

## Fact notes (what's solid, what to leave out)
1. **Pisa:** the committee chose controlled "underexcavation": small amounts of soil removed from beneath the **north** side, because touching the south side was too dangerous; handed back June 2001. Straightened by about **40 cm** (sources say 38–44 cm): say "about forty centimetres". Leave out the student-paper details (exact start date). Sources: ICE, Ingenia (Burland), Burland/Jamiolkowski/Viggiani paper (Soils and Foundations), Britannica.
2. **Rumford:** made fireplaces **shallower with a smaller opening**, angled the side walls and streamlined the throat with a smoke shelf; essays 1796 and 1798; London houses became smoke-free. **250 London fireplaces rebuilt in two months** has only one source: check it before using. Sources: Wikipedia "Rumford fireplace", Library of Congress blog (Jefferson and the Rumford fireplace), Monticello, American Heritage.
3. **Paxton:** his daughter **Annie** stood on a *Victoria* water lily leaf at Chatsworth (Illustrated London News engraving, 17 Nov 1849). Her age varies (5 or 8), so don't say it. The ribs fed into the Chatsworth lily house, then the Crystal Palace (1851). Sources: Wikipedia "Joseph Paxton", The Garden History blog, Museums of History NSW, FEE.
4. **Gaudí:** hanging chain or rope models with weights, viewed in a mirror, for the Colònia Güell church; 1:10 model (lost; replica at the Sagrada Família museum). Don't claim "ten years" or "birdshot" until checked. Sources: Dezeen (2026), Wikipedia "Funicular curve", Springer *Nexus* paper.
5. **Cook:** his journal, 13 April 1769: he had it served at the cabin table and let the officers eat it; once the men see their superiors value it, "it becomes the finest stuff in the world." "The crew sent a delegation" is secondary only: leave it out. Sources: Cook's journal (State Library of NSW transcription, Project Gutenberg), Australian National Maritime Museum.
6. **Mehmed:** 22 April 1453, oxen and men dragged **70–80** ships over the hill behind Galata on greased logs, relaunching behind the chain. Say "about seventy". Sources: Britannica, Wikipedia "Fall of Constantinople", Stanton *Medieval Maritime Warfare*.
7. **Bertram:** a dummy water pipeline with dummy pump houses, built at a pace that made the attack look weeks away; about 600 tanks disguised as lorries with "Sunshields". The 200-mile length has only one source: leave it out. Sources: Wikipedia "Operation Bertram", IWM, historyofwar.org.
8. **Halley:** weighted barrels of air refilled the bell; he and companions stayed down about 90 minutes at nine to ten fathoms (his own account). The date is messy (1691 design, published 1716–17): say "three hundred years ago". Sources: Wikipedia "History of underwater diving", Royal Society picture library, St Andrews MacTutor.
