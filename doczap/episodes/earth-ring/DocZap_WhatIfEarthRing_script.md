# Doc Zap, "What if…": Earth had a ring like Saturn

**Why this one:** it's the most unique of the five. A "what if" that turns out to be real: in 2024, geologists found evidence that Earth really had a ring about 466 million years ago, and that it may have plunged the planet into an ice age. Very few people know it.

```
What if Earth had a ring... like Saturn?
It might have. Four hundred and sixty-six million years ago.
Picture it: a glowing arch across the sky, every single night.
You see, a giant asteroid flew too close.
Earth's gravity tore it apart... into a ring of rocks around the equator.
Then the rocks rained down... for tens of millions of years.
In twenty twenty-four, scientists spotted a strange clue. Twenty-one craters from that era...
every single one in the tropics. Even though most land was outside them.
But that beautiful ring may have done something worse.
Its shadow may have helped cool the planet... into one of the coldest ice ages in half a billion years.
The most beautiful sky Earth ever had... may have helped freeze it.
So next time someone asks...
```

## Fact check (2+ sources per claim)
- **About 466 million years ago; 21 impact craters, all within 30° of the equator, though over 70 % of continental crust was outside that band.** Sources: Monash University press release; Tomkins et al., *Earth and Planetary Science Letters* 646 (2024); EOS; Newsweek.
- **An asteroid passed too close, broke apart (tidal forces / Roche limit), formed a debris ring around the equator, and the material fell to Earth over time.** Sources: Monash; Tomkins in The Conversation; Space.com; TechSpot.
- **The ring lasted tens of millions of years** (at least 40 million years per EOS). Sources: Space.com; The Conversation (Tomkins); EOS.
- **The ring's shadow may have contributed to the Hirnantian ice age, one of the coldest periods in the last 500 million years.** Sources: Monash; Smithsonian; Space.com. This is a hypothesis, so the script says "may have" each time.
- **Published in 2024.** Sources: Monash; EarthSky (Sept 12 2024, peer-reviewed).
- **How certain is it?** A hypothesis. The authors say "may have". Outside scientists are cautious: Birger Schmitz says the data aren't yet enough to say Earth indeed had rings (Smithsonian, Space.com), and Elizabeth Catlos questions some crater dates (EOS). So the script says "might have" and "may have".
- **Doubt review (a fresh fact-checker):** changed "probably did" → "might have", "millions" → "tens of millions", "near the equator / far from it" → "in the tropics / outside them" (the band is within 30° of the equator), "cooled the whole planet" → "helped cool the planet", and "frozen it" → "helped freeze it" (the Hirnantian ice sheets covered mainly the southern continents, not the whole Earth). The ice age came about 445 million years ago, around 20 million years after the ring formed.
- **Left out:** the odds figure. Space.com reports "1 in 25 million", but Monash's three-sided-coin comparison works out to a different number, so it isn't used. Also left out: the 8 °C drop (one source).
